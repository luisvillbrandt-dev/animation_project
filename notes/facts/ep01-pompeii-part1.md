### 1. Vesuvius: shape, distance, direction

1a. Two peaks (Somma rim + cone), as today
- Now: drawing has two bumps on top.
- Rating: DISPUTED for 79 AD (not shown to be wrong, but not safe either). The familiar "Somma rim around an inner cone" outline is documented only from 1631 on, per a Scientific American geology blog. Ancient evidence disagrees: one Pompeii wall painting shows a single summit with forest and vines; a Herculaneum painting shows twin summits (a possible explanation is viewpoint: an older caldera hidden from Pompeii). Strabo (1st c.) describes a mostly flat, barren, ash-grey top. Another blog (VolcanoCafe, hobbyist) argues the Somma rim only stood higher than the cone after 79. Wikipedia's Vesuvius page says the wall painting suggests one summit at the time and that the Gran Cono was built during the 79 eruption (secondary source, LIKELY at best).
- Sources opened:
  - https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/pompeii-a-geological-movie-review-introducing-the-main-character
  - https://geo.mtu.edu/volcanoes/boris/mirror/mirrored_html/VESUVIO_79.html (no named author, 1996 mirror; weak, but it reports published research)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Vesuvius
  - https://www.volcanocafe.org/the-enigma-of-the-79-ad-eruption-of-vesuvius/ (weak)
- Decision: CHANGE. Draw ONE summit: a broad cone with a flat or gently rounded top (fits both Strabo and the Pompeii painting). Remove the notch between two humps. A viewer who knows only today's Vesuvius may expect two peaks, but a viewer who knows the research can fairly call two peaks anachronistic; one flat-topped summit is the safer picture.

1b. Brown bare mountain
- Now: solid brown (#8c6a55) all over.
- Rating: LIKELY. Roman writers describe the slopes as covered in vineyards and gardens/forest, with a craggy, bare, ash-grey top (Scientific American blog; Wikipedia Vesuvius; both secondary reports of Strabo).
- Decision: CHANGE (cheap). Muted olive green on the lower two thirds, grey-brown on the top third. Keep one flat colour per zone, same outline.

1c. Smoke wisp on the summit before the eruption
- Now: three cream puffs above the summit in the calm scene.
- Rating: UNSUPPORTED. I found no source saying Vesuvius smoked before the eruption. The 79 article only reports minor earthquakes in the four days before, which locals did not take as a warning; the script itself says "a normal day" and "Nobody here knows what this is".
- Source opened: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD (the article does not say whether people knew it was a volcano)
- Decision: CHANGE. Remove the puffs in the baker and calm-street scenes. First smoke appears only with the column.

1d. Distance and direction from Pompeii
- Now: mountain fills the background right behind the roofs, looks very close.
- Rating: LIKELY. Pompeii is about 8 km from Vesuvius (Wikipedia, Pompeii). The plume was carried south-southeast and Pompeii lay on the main fallout axis (Montana Tech page; Wikipedia 79 article says fallout went south toward Pompeii). So from Pompeii the volcano lies roughly north-northwest. That bearing is my own inference from those statements, not stated in a source. Peak height today is 1,281 m (Wikipedia, Vesuvius).
- Sources opened: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii ; https://geo.mtu.edu/volcanoes/boris/mirror/mirrored_html/VESUVIO_79.html
- Decision: KEEP the idea (mountain large on the horizon behind the houses). Keep its base wide and low (gentle slopes), not a steep triangle.

1e. Escape direction (harbor)
- The sea is south/west of Pompeii (city on a coastal plateau about 40 m above sea level, drops to the sea on south and partly west; the Sarno mouth was the port). So the harbor lies on the side away from the volcano.
- Rating: LIKELY (Wikipedia, Pompeii; plus the inference above).
- Decision: CHANGE (free). In the "neighbors running" scene, let people run away from the mountain: toward the viewer or to the side opposite the volcano, never toward it.

### 2. Streets and houses

2a. Paving, sidewalks, stepping stones
- Now: flat tan ground with light pill shapes.
- Rating: CONFIRMED. By 79 most of the street grid was paved with lava stone; pavements were raised kerbs to keep rainwater out of houses; about 316 big stepping stones crossed wet streets, spaced so cart wheels fit between them (AJA review of Poehler, The Traffic Systems of Pompeii; AJA abstract of Poehler and Crowther, "Paving Pompeii"; popular recap in My Modern Met). Deep wheel ruts are shown in a photo caption on via Stabiana. The colour of the stone is not given on the pages I opened; "dark" is my assumption, not verified.
- Sources opened:
  - https://ajaonline.org/?p=4839
  - https://ajaonline.org/?p=1360
  - https://mymodernmet.com/pompeii-roads (popular, weak, but agrees)
- Decision: CHANGE. (1) Ground: darker than the walls, built from irregular flat stones, not pills. (2) Add a raised kerb strip along the foot of the houses (one extra rectangle). (3) Add two or three squarish stepping stones across the street (also nice for pumice piling up around them later). (4) Optional: two parallel cart-rut lines.

2b. Roofs
- Now: red triangle gable facing the viewer, small overhang.
- Rating: LIKELY for "pitched roofs of fired clay tiles (flat tegulae with half-round imbrices over the joints)" (Wikipedia, Imbrex and tegula). Roof pitch angle: UNSUPPORTED, I found no number. Tile colour: not stated on the page I opened (terracotta is a reasonable guess, unverified). Whether a street-facing triangular gable is typical: UNSUPPORTED. A 2024 study infers a sloping roof with a wooden attic at one Pompeii room, no angle given. Atrium houses had a central roof opening and the roof sloped toward it (Wikipedia, Domus).
- Sources opened: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imbrex_and_tegula ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domus ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full
- Decision: CHANGE (small). Make roofs low and shallow, eave line parallel to the street, terracotta red-orange, with a few horizontal tile-row lines. The steep temple-style triangle reads as "Greek temple / modern cottage". A shallow pitch is also easier to show piling up with pumice.

2c. Windows and facades
- Now: two large symmetric windows per house, uniform orange walls.
- Rating: LIKELY. City houses had few windows on the street side because glass was scarce; light came from the atrium (Wikipedia, Domus). Facades were plastered and painted; red and yellow were common (source is a novelist's blog, weak: https://www.elodieharper.com/post/pompeii-street-scenes). A scholarly chapter on Pompeian facade colours exists (Fridell Anter and Weilguni, 2018) but I could not open it.
- Decision: CHANGE (small). Fewer and smaller windows, set higher on the wall; one or two blank wall areas; keep warm plaster colours (ochre, red-brown, cream). Optional: a red band along the lower wall.

2d. Shop front (taberna) and door
- Now: tall arched doorway with oven glow.
- Rating: LIKELY. Shops opened to the street with a wide doorway, closed at night by wooden boards/shutters set in a groove or socket in a lava threshold; lintels were wooden (carbonized in one case). A small roof over the pavement on beams in one example. Sources: pompeii.pictures entries 6.16.10 and 9.11.8 (a Pompeii documentation site quoting excavation reports; decent but not academic) and the novelist blog above. Wikipedia (Pompeii) says thermopolia opened straight onto the street with a counter. Arches are known in Pompeii but I found no source for arched shop openings.
- Sources opened: https://pompeii.pictures/R6/6%2016%2010.htm ; https://pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/R9/9 11 08.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii
- Decision: CHANGE. Replace the arch with a wide rectangular opening (flat top, one thick lintel line), with a stone threshold step. Inside: dark with the oven glow. Optional: one or two shutter boards leaning at the side. For "family at the door": a narrow rectangular house door, flat top, dark opening.

### 3. The bakery

3a. Oven
- Now: orange half-oval glow inside a doorway.
- Rating: LIKELY (sources weak). Ovens were built of basalt/volcanic stone and sand, described as a vaulted chamber (nunc.ch, a bread-history article, single source); brick, arched, large (worldhistory.org); a travel-guide page says "domed" with a wide mouth and a flue (weak). All agree on a large masonry oven with an arched mouth; the exact dome shape is not firmly sourced. Fuel: wood and olive stones; it reached baking heat in 2 to 3 hours and was ready when the soot burned off the vault (nunc.ch).
- Sources opened: https://nunc.ch/en/can-pompeiis-bread-be-recreated/ ; https://www.worldhistory.org/image/11294/the-bakery-of-popidius-priscus-in-pompeii/ ; https://audiala.com/en/italy/pompeii/bakery-ix-1-3 (weak)
- Decision: CHANGE (small). Draw a stone dome (rounded hump) with a small arched mouth; orange glow only in the mouth. Do not make the whole doorway an oven. Keep the warm glow.

3b. Mills
- Rating: LIKELY. Bakeries had several large mills of porous lava stone, often four, turned by donkeys, with a stable nearby (worldhistory.org; The Past magazine on the Insula 10 bakery; audiala, weak). The exact shape (funnel-shaped upper stone) appeared only in a search snippet I did not open: not verified.
- Sources opened: https://the-past.com/?p=68673 ; worldhistory.org page above
- Decision: optional ADD. One squat grey stone drum (a flat rounded rectangle with a wooden beam sticking out) in the bakery corner. Skip if the scene feels crowded.

3c. Bread
- Rating: LIKELY. Carbonized loaves from the Modestus bakery: round, about 20 cm across, about 10 cm high, top divided into 6 or 8 wedge segments by radial cuts; some stamped with the baker's name. 81 loaves found in the oven; only 6 survive (nunc.ch, single source I opened; other pages seen only as snippets).
- Decision: ADD (very cheap, high authenticity). Bread as a round brown loaf with 6 to 8 radial lines, like a pie. Avoid baguettes or long modern loaves.

3d. Baker's workplace
- Rating: LIKELY. Kneading in deep troughs of rough volcanic stone; stiff dough (nunc.ch). Pompeii had 31 or more bakeries, each with oven, mills and a sales counter (Wikipedia, Pompeii; the Popidius Priscus bakery had no shop, so not all had a counter; counts vary by source: 31, about 35, 40+).
- Decision: KEEP the baker next to the oven. Optional: a flat stone counter in the shop opening.

### 5. Eruption visuals and timing

5a. Column "like a tree"
- Now: three small cream circles.
- Rating: CONFIRMED that Pliny the Younger compares the cloud to a pine tree: a very tall trunk that spread into branches; colour sometimes white, sometimes dirty and blotched, depending on how much earth and ash it carried (Pliny, Letter 6.16, Attalus translation; Wikipedia, 79 article agrees). Note: Pliny watched from Misenum, about 30 km away; from Pompeii (8 km) the cloud would loom much larger and lean over the city (plume went south-southeast). Peak height about 33 km (Wikipedia; 27 km in the early stage per a search snippet of Sigurdsson 1985, not opened).
- Sources opened: https://www.attalus.org/old/pliny6.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD
- Decision: CHANGE. Draw a tall, narrow trunk rising from the summit past the top of the frame, with a flat umbrella-shaped crown spreading sideways (the Mediterranean umbrella pine is the model). Colour: pale grey-white with a few darker grey blotches, not black. Lean it slightly toward the viewer's side if possible.

5b. When it began
- Rating: CONFIRMED for "early afternoon". Pliny's mother pointed out the cloud at the "seventh hour" (6.16; Roman hours counted from sunrise, so roughly 1 pm, depending on the season). Sigurdsson et al. 1985 abstract says the Plinian eruption began shortly after noon on 24 August; Sparice et al. 2024 (Frontiers in Earth Science) put the start of white pumice fall at about 1 pm; the Montana Tech page says about 13:00.
- Sources opened: https://www.attalus.org/old/pliny6.html ; https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/gsofacpubs/1114 ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full
- Decision: KEEP "just after midday". Sky at the baker and first street scenes: still bright. Fits the script.

5c. Pumice: colour, size, duration
- Rating: CONFIRMED that white pumice fell first, for about 7 hours (Sigurdsson et al. 1985 abstract; Sparice et al. 2024), then grey pumice; the whole Plinian fall lasted about 18 to 19 hours (Sparice 2024; Carey and Sigurdsson 1987 abstract). Size: white pieces up to about 3 cm, grey up to about 10 cm (Wikipedia, 79 article; single secondary source for sizes, so LIKELY). Fall rate about 15 cm per hour in open ground (Sparice 2024). Pompeii deposit about 2.8 m (Wikipedia); a 2024 study mentions 3.1 to 3.3 m in one alley. Pliny also notes ash, pumice and blackened, cracked stones on ships near the mountain (6.16).
- Sources opened: https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/gsofacpubs/1114 ; https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/gsofacpubs/1107 ; Sparice and Wikipedia pages above
- Decision: KEEP "small white stones" (matches). Draw pumice as small off-white angular chips and dots (cream-white, not grey), falling at a slant. Later scenes: a pale layer building up on street, kerb, stepping stones and roof; by the beam scene the pile is knee-to-waist high outside (about 40 to 60 cm after a few hours, more later). Optionally switch some chips to grey and bigger in the last scenes.

5d. Roofs collapsing under weight
- Rating: CONFIRMED. Most roof collapses at Pompeii happened a few hours into the Plinian phase while white pumice was piling up, at roughly 40 to 60 cm of deposit; some roofs held because they were sloped and shed the pumice (Sparice et al. 2024). A news report on the same research says roofs gave way under pumice weight and some people sheltering inside were the first victims. The same paper argues earthquakes (not pumice load) caused some wall failures later, which fits the script's "ground shakes again".
- Sources opened: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full ; https://www.9colonne.it/471552/unraveling-the-past-ingv-researchers-reconstructed-seismicity-effects-of-the-79-ad-eruption-of-mt-vesuvius ; https://geo.mtu.edu/volcanoes/boris/mirror/mirrored_html/VESUVIO_79.html
- Decision: KEEP the creaking beam scene. Visual: roof tiles pale-covered, a thin line of dust falling from the beam, a slightly tilted beam line. Do not show the collapse itself as certain for this baker (fiction).

5e. When and how the sky went dark
- Rating: LIKELY for "gets darker through the afternoon and evening"; UNSUPPORTED for exact times at Pompeii. There is no surviving eyewitness account from Pompeii. The darkness descriptions in Pliny are from Stabiae (6.16, morning of 25 August, "darker than any night", lamps and torches used) and Misenum (6.20: black cloud, flashes like lightning, darkness like a closed room, sun later blood-red). A Montana Tech page says dense plume and heavy fallout gave complete darkness in the fallout zone (unauthored, weak). Pompeii lay on the fallout axis, so darkness there was likely at least as early as at Misenum, but the hour is not documented.
- Sources opened: https://www.attalus.org/old/pliny6.html ; https://geo.mtu.edu/volcanoes/boris/mirror/mirrored_html/VESUVIO_79.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD
- Decision: KEEP the plan (bright, then ash grey, then dark) with these notes: bright warm sky for the baker and the calm street; grey-yellow, dim light for the running neighbors (about 2 pm); very dark for the family at the door and the beam scene, lit only by an orange oil lamp (lamps are in Pliny 6.16). Do not make the first column black; Pliny says white or dirty at first. A red sun (Pliny 6.20) is documented only for Misenum on day 2; skip it.

5f. Pillows on heads
- Rating: CONFIRMED in Pliny 6.16 (Pliny the Elder's party at Stabiae tied pillows to their heads with cloths against falling stones). Applies to people outside during the stone fall; not documented for Pompeii itself, so LIKELY for the neighbors.
- Source opened: https://www.attalus.org/old/pliny6.html
- Decision: ADD (very cheap, memorable). A small cushion shape tied on the head of a running stickman.

5g. Date debate (24 August vs autumn)
- Rating: DISPUTED. The 24 August date comes from manuscript copies of Pliny's letter (a 1508 printing); manuscripts vary (24 Aug, 30 Oct, 1 Nov, 23 Nov are all reported). Evidence for autumn: autumn fruit and braziers (since 1797), victims in heavy clothing, sealed wine jars, a 2007 wind study, and a charcoal graffito in a house under renovation dated "XVI K Nov" (17 October, year unknown) found in 2018; Pompeii's director then proposed 24 October. A 2022 study proposed late October (24 Oct to 1 Nov in one summary, 24-25 Oct in another) and was challenged. Nothing is settled; the graffito alone does not prove the eruption date (the archaeology.org report calls it only a challenge to the old date and is second-hand, via the BBC).
- Sources opened: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD ; https://archaeology.org/news/2018/10/16/181016-pompeii-date-inscription/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii
- Decision: KEEP the script (it gives no date). Drawing: avoid season clues that commit to one side: no green summer foliage in the foreground, no cloaks, no thin summer clothing; muted olive on the mountain is fine for both. Sun at about 1 pm is fine either way; make it pale and low-contrast as the column grows.

### Key timeline used for this check (all times approximate)
- About 1 pm, day 1: Plinian eruption starts after a small opening explosion; white pumice begins to fall on Pompeii about the same time (Sigurdsson 1985 abstract; Tulane lecture page; Sparice 2024).
- White pumice about 7 h, then grey pumice; whole fall about 18-19 h (Sigurdsson 1985; Sparice 2024).
- About 15 cm of pumice per hour; most roof collapses a few hours in, at 40-60 cm (Sparice 2024). The Tulane page estimates roofs at risk by about 5 pm (estimate, not a record).
- Early morning, day 2: first surge to reach Pompeii (surge 4): about 7 am (Sigurdsson 1985 abstract); about 6:30 (Tulane page); about 8 am for the caldera-forming current (Sparice 2024). Safe summary: "early morning, around 7".
- Victims: 1,150 found at Pompeii; 394 in the pumice-fall layer (died mostly when roofs and walls collapsed); 650 in the surge deposits (day 2); most inhabitants left during the first phase (Luongo et al. 2003 abstract).

### CLAIMS

1. "By tomorrow morning, this whole city will be gone." (line 1) | timing LIKELY, "gone" overstated | REWORD
- Timing: the surges that killed those still in Pompeii arrived in the early morning of day 2 (about 7 am), after about 3 m of pumice had already buried the city (Sigurdsson 1985 abstract; Luongo 2003 abstract; Tulane). The Wikipedia 79 article attributes the destruction of Pompeii to surges 4 and 5 and a surge layer about 1.8 m thick. Because the line is relative ("tomorrow morning"), the open date debate (24 Aug vs. autumn) does not matter.
- "Gone" overstates: the city was buried, not erased (it was excavated again later; that last point is from general knowledge, not an opened source, so do not rely on it). Most people had also already left.

7. "Nobody here knows what this is." (line 8) | LIKELY for ordinary people, DISPUTED for educated people | REWORD
- Some ancient writers (Strabo, Vitruvius, Diodorus) seem to have understood that the mountain was volcanic in nature (Scientific American geology blog), but they did not treat it as a threat. Strabo describes burned-looking rock and compares it with Etna. Pliny 6.16 says even observers across the bay could not tell which mountain the cloud rose from. Tremors in the days before were not taken as warnings (Wikipedia 79 article). The Harvard exhibition page says residents were "taken by surprise". ### Part 2: can it reveal what happened to each choice?
- Option B (stay): documented at population level. Deaths under collapsing roofs during the pumice fall (394 victims) and deaths in the day-2 surges (650 victims). Enough for an honest reveal: many who stayed died when roofs gave way, or when the surge hit at about 7 am.
- Option A (run): documented that most inhabitants left in the first phase and survived (Luongo 2003; Western Australian Museum); secondary sources say most escapees went by road, many to Naples, Cumae, Ostia, Puteoli. By SEA from Pompeii: not documented, and the Stabiae attempt failed. If Option A stays "harbor", Part 2 cannot honestly say people reached boats; it can say the sea route was blocked in this bay and most survivors left by road. If Option A becomes "run out of the city", Part 2 can say most who left early survived. Individual outcomes cannot be known (no record of any baker's fate).
- Both options carry real danger on day 1: leaving meant walking through falling stones (pillows) in a dim afternoon.
